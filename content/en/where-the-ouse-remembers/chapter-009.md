---
chapter: 9
title: "— A MAN WHO LISTENED"
---
Adrian visited the Ward house on a Sunday afternoon carrying a bottle of whisky and no property documents.

Arthur met him at the door.

“My answer's still no.”

“I came to ask about the performance tradition Eleanor used at the hotel.”

Arthur narrowed his eyes.

Ten minutes later they were in the archive room.

Thirty minutes later Arthur had taken down the concertina.

An hour later Margaret sent Eleanor to buy more bread because the two men had decided whisky required supper.

Adrian understood an important truth about Arthur quickly: if you argued about money, he closed. If you asked what mattered, he opened.

Arthur explained the Mystery Plays, the old craft guild traditions, local mummers' fragments, songs and comic recitations he had collected over decades. He complained that visitors came to York wanting “history” but were often given costumes without context.

Adrian listened.

Not politely. Seriously.

“What if the townhouses had small performances?” he asked. “Not every night. Not dinner theatre. Something curated. Local performers, workshops, stories tied to the neighbourhood.”

Arthur's suspicion remained, but curiosity had entered beside it.

“My house still isn't for sale.”

“Understood.”

“Don't think whisky changes that.”

“I brought the cheap bottle for exactly that reason.”

Arthur laughed.

Eleanor, watching from the kitchen door, realised Adrian had accomplished in one afternoon what most people failed to do in years: he had made her father feel understood without agreeing with him.

Before leaving, Adrian accepted a bag of potatoes from Arthur's allotment as if it were a diplomatic gift.

After the door closed, Arthur looked at Eleanor.

“Your boss is dangerous.”

“Why?”

“He listens.”

“Terrible habit.”

Arthur tried not to smile.

Then he said, “You sang well.”

Eleanor went still.

Praise from Arthur was rare enough to qualify as weather.

“Thank you.”

He nodded and returned to the back room.

That evening, Eleanor cried in the car before driving home.

Not from sadness.

From the strange pain of receiving something you had spent years pretending not to need.
