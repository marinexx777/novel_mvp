---
chapter: 2
title: "— A HOUSE DIVIDED"
---
The Ward house had three bedrooms, one sitting room, a kitchen too small for five adults and opinions large enough for twelve.

Within ten minutes of Eleanor's arrival, every old family argument had found fresh oxygen.

Daniel and Caroline had sold their semi-detached house because their fourteen-year-old son, Oliver, had been offered a place at an expensive independent school. Daniel worked for a utility company and earned well, but school fees, a mortgage and Caroline's talent for spending money before it arrived had left them anxious.

Then a hotel group had approached Arthur about buying the Ward house and the adjoining property. The site, they said, would be ideal for a “historic York townhouse experience.” The offer included new-build flats for several family members and a cash payment large enough to change all their lives.

Arthur had thrown the representative out.

“He wants to turn this place into a stage set for people who think history is wallpaper,” he said.

Caroline folded her arms. “Or he wants to pay us a very fair price for a building that's falling apart.”

“It's my building.”

“And one day it will be the family's.”

Arthur's eyes sharpened. “I'm not dead yet.”

Silence fell.

Eleanor had not even removed her coat.

Caroline looked at the suitcases again. “Surely Michael can arrange somewhere for you. He was doing very well, wasn't he?”

Eleanor swallowed.

Jamie, who had been glaring at Caroline, turned to his sister. “Where is Michael?”

Lily answered before Eleanor could.

“Daddy's dead.”

The room changed.

Margaret reached for Lily immediately. Daniel went pale. Even Caroline's face collapsed into shock.

Arthur stood.

“You didn't tell us?”

Eleanor looked at him. “You weren't speaking to me.”

“You still had a mother. Brothers. A grandmother.”

“I know.”

“How?”

“Car accident.”

That was the clean version.

It left out the business debts. The mortgaged London flat. The company that had unravelled after Michael's death. The business partner who had taken key clients. The woman who appeared with a little boy and a DNA report claiming Michael had fathered him.

It left out the knowledge that Eleanor's marriage had apparently contained another life.

Arthur's anger shifted, but it did not disappear.

“You chose that life,” he said.

Margaret hissed, “Arthur.”

“No. She wanted London. She wanted that man. She wanted to leave work and be kept.”

Eleanor flinched.

Years earlier, Arthur had wanted her to train formally in local performance and community theatre, following a tradition he had loved since youth: the York Mystery Plays, mummers' pieces, folk recitations, anything that belonged to streets rather than television studios. Eleanor had refused and studied hospitality instead. Then she moved south, married Michael and eventually stopped working to raise Lily.

Arthur had treated each decision as a rejection of him.

“I stayed home with my child,” Eleanor said. “I didn't commit a crime.”

“I educated you so you could choose a life, not disappear inside somebody else's.”

“It was my choice.”

“Then why are you here?”

That struck where he intended.

Eleanor grabbed the suitcase handle.

Margaret caught her arm. “Nobody is going anywhere tonight.”

Caroline opened her mouth, probably to discuss beds.

Jamie cut her off. “Ellie and Lily can have my room. I'll sleep at a mate's.”

Daniel looked miserable. “We can move some furniture—”

“No,” Eleanor said. Her voice was calmer now. “I'll find a hotel.”

“Don't be ridiculous,” Margaret said.

But Eleanor had already understood something painful: coming home did not mean the past made space for you.

At the gate, a man carrying a paper bag stopped and stared.

“Ellie?”

She turned.

Peter Hale had lived two doors away when they were children. He had been in her class from primary school until sixth form, had once broken his wrist jumping from a wall because she dared him, and had loved her with a patience she found inconvenient at seventeen.

He looked older now, naturally. Broader. Calm.

“Peter,” she said.

His eyes moved to Lily, the luggage, her face.

He did not ask questions.

“Need a hand?”

For the first time that day, Eleanor almost smiled.

“Yes.”
