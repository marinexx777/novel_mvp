---
chapter: 4
title: "— ARTHUR'S BOXES"
---
Arthur Ward kept his favourite things in a narrow back room everyone else called the junk room.

It contained old programmes, handwritten scripts, tin whistles, a battered concertina, costume pieces, a frame drum, boxes of photographs and folders labelled with dates going back forty years.

To Arthur, it was an archive.

After Eleanor left, he sat among it for a long time.

He had joined amateur community theatre at fifteen. His mentor, Alfie Mercer, had taught him that local performance did not have to be grand to matter. A street play, a comic monologue, a folk song, a Mystery Play scene rehearsed in a church hall—these were ways people remembered where they came from.

“Keep it alive,” Alfie used to say. “Once something becomes only a museum piece, people admire it instead of using it.”

Most of the old group had scattered. Some died. Some moved. Some stopped performing when audiences shrank.

Arthur became an English teacher, but never entirely stopped.

He opened a folder and found Eleanor's childhood certificates.

At nine, she had performed in a children's pageant near the city walls and forgotten half her lines. Arthur had fed them to her from offstage. At twelve, she could sing every verse of an old Yorkshire ballad in a voice clear enough to silence a room.

At sixteen, she announced it was embarrassing.

At eighteen, she refused to study performance.

At twenty-four, she moved to London.

At twenty-nine, she stopped working.

Arthur had told himself his anger came from principle.

Alone among the boxes, he admitted that much of it came from hurt.

Margaret found him holding a photograph of Eleanor in costume.

“You miss her,” she said.

“I miss the child she was.”

“No. You miss the woman too. You're simply bad at it.”

Arthur grunted.

“Michael's dead,” Margaret said gently. “Their home is gone. She has a little girl who barely speaks. If this is the moment you choose to win an old argument, you'll lose something much bigger.”

Arthur looked at the photograph again.

“Did she tell you everything?”

“Enough.”

He hated that Eleanor had suffered in London. He hated more that she had suffered without calling him.

But he also knew why.

He had made pride feel like a locked gate.

In the hotel, Eleanor dreamed of childhood.

She was walking the walls with Arthur, listening to him turn every stone into a story. Then the dream shifted. Michael stood beside a pregnant woman Eleanor could not quite see. Lily called from somewhere behind a locked door.

She woke with her heart racing.

Her phone showed a message from Peter.

Found three flats within twenty minutes of your parents. One has terrible wallpaper but a decent kitchen. I can show you tomorrow.

Eleanor stared at the screen.

There are kinds of love, she thought, that arrive with promises.

And kinds that arrive with property listings.
