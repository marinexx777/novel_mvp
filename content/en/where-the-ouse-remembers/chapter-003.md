chapter: 3
title: "— THE BOY WHO STAYED"
---
Peter drove a six-year-old Volkswagen and apologised for the mess as if Eleanor had arrived expecting a chauffeur.

There were programming manuals in the back seat, a reusable coffee cup on the floor and a packet of mint humbugs in the door pocket.

“You're still in tech?” she asked.

“Software systems for a logistics company. Deeply glamorous. Sometimes a spreadsheet crashes and everyone screams.”

Lily sat behind them, silent again.

Peter did not push her.

He booked Eleanor into a modest hotel near Micklegate, carried the luggage upstairs and waited while she settled Lily under the duvet.

At the door he said, “I'll take you both for dinner when she wakes up. Unless you want to be alone.”

“I don't know what I want.”

“Then I'll text. No pressure.”

That was Peter: present without taking up too much room.

Later, after Lily fell asleep, Eleanor called her mother and finally told the fuller story.

Michael's startup had been growing quickly but surviving on loans and confidence. Their London flat had been used as security. After his death, a co-founder shifted contracts into a new company. Then Nora Lewis—Michael's head of sales—arrived with a small boy and documents claiming Michael was the father.

Michael's parents accepted the child almost instantly. They had always wanted a grandson. Eleanor, exhausted and humiliated, did not fight. She sold what she could, settled debts and left London with enough money to survive for a while.

Margaret cried quietly on the phone.

“Why didn't you tell me?”

“Because I could hear Dad saying I told you so before I even called.”

“He wouldn't.”

“He would think it.”

“That isn't the same.”

Sometimes, Eleanor thought, marriage lasted because women became experts at translating men into kinder languages.

At six, Peter met them downstairs. He took them to a family restaurant near the river where the menu leaned heavily into Yorkshire comfort: roast beef, pies, proper gravy, sticky toffee pudding.

Lily ate three chips before Peter asked whether her rabbit had a name.

“No.”

“Very mysterious. Is it in witness protection?”

Lily looked at him.

“What?”

“It means he might be hiding from dangerous carrots.”

A tiny smile appeared.

Eleanor looked away before Peter saw what that did to her.

After dinner he walked them along the Ouse. The evening light rested on the water. Tour boats moved beneath the bridges. The city carried the usual mix of students, tourists, office workers and people who had lived there long enough to be irritated by all three.

“You really staying?” Peter asked.

“Yes.”

“What will you do?”

“Find a flat. Find work. Put Lily back in school when she's ready. Try not to destroy my family.”

“Ambitious first week.”

She laughed.

Peter stopped near the railings.

“I heard about the house offer.”

“Everyone has.”

“Your dad won't sell easily.”

“He thinks buildings have souls.”

Peter looked at the river. “Maybe some do.”

Eleanor studied him.

“You're still romantic.”

“No. I work in software. Romance violates several security policies.”

She smiled again.

Peter had once wanted to follow her to university. He missed the grades for his first choice, and by the time he found another route, Eleanor was already falling in love with Michael.

For years, she had assumed Peter's feelings had faded.

Now she wondered whether some things did not fade so much as learn good manners.
