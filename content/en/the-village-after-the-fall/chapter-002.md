---
chapter: 2
title: "— EIGHT THOUSAND POUNDS"
---
Daniel arrived the next morning carrying a supermarket bag, two jars of chutney and a debit card.

"There's eight thousand in there," he said.

Martin stared at him. "Eight thousand what?"

"Pounds, you idiot. I haven't started minting euros in the barn."

Daniel's humour had always arrived dry and late, like a match struck after everyone had left the room.

"I can't take it."

"You can."

"What about Helen?"

Daniel's wife had never forgiven Martin for treating her as a provincial nuisance during his successful years.

"She doesn't know."

"Then absolutely not."

Daniel pushed the card across the table. "You paid for our roof when it leaked. You bought Mum's stairlift. You never made me ask. Don't become proud only when pride is expensive."

Martin hated him for being right.

He used the money to lease a narrow café on the edge of Bristol, in a street busy enough for lunch trade but unfashionable enough to be affordable. He had run a restaurant with Claire in their younger years, before property seduced him with the promise that money could reproduce faster than people could cook it.

He named the place The Willow Table.

Daniel supplied vegetables from Somerset: heritage tomatoes, young courgettes, runner beans, eggs, mushrooms, herbs and asparagus from neighbours who were glad of a cash buyer. Martin hired a cook and one server, painted the walls himself, and put a handwritten board outside.

COUNTRY FOOD. FAIR PRICES. NO NONSENSE.

At first, customers came because the portions were generous. They returned because the produce tasted of something.

Martin rediscovered the pleasure of receiving money for a meal that physically existed rather than for a drawing of a building that might exist three years later.

The café did well.

Success, however, remained dangerous to him.

Within four months he was already sketching expansion plans on napkins.

Daniel noticed.

"You promised yourself one café."

"I didn't promise. I considered."

"That's how it starts."

Martin ignored him.

A former army friend, Tom Barrett, now worked with a small cidery in Herefordshire. Another old friend knew bottling and distribution. Martin saw an opportunity for a restaurant-label cider brand called Three Fellows, built around an old saying his father liked: one fence post falls; three hold each other up.

The product was decent, the label charming, the price sensible. Pubs and independent shops began ordering it. Martin bundled sample bottles with restaurant catering contracts and commissioned seasonal labels for weddings, alumni dinners and local festivals.

For the first time since the collapse, he had enough cash to repay Daniel.

He transferred the eight thousand pounds back, plus interest Daniel had not asked for.

Helen discovered what her husband had done only after the money returned.

She arrived at The Willow Table furious anyway.

"If Daniel funded this place, then Daniel owns part of it."

"He loaned me money. I've repaid it."

"With what? Money you made using our vegetables, our van and his time."

Daniel stood behind her, pale with embarrassment.

Martin felt the old rage rise—the rage that confused being challenged with being humiliated.

He almost told Helen to get out.

Then he remembered the glass swan.

"Fine," he said. "Twenty percent of the café profit goes to Daniel while he supplies the produce. In writing. Transparent accounts. If either side wants out, we agree a clean exit."

Helen blinked.

She had expected a fight.

So had Martin.

That night, alone in the kitchen, he realised something uncomfortable.

A year earlier he would have considered compromise weakness.

Now it felt like not setting fire to a room simply because he disliked the wallpaper.

He was learning.

Slowly.
